# Boeing 737 MAX and MCAS

## The system

The system here is the 737 MAX type design and the certification that approved it: Boeing designs and holds the design, and the FAA certifies it. The order that makes the two one governance is FAA type certification under 14 CFR, under which Boeing as applicant shows the design complies, and the FAA delegated parts of that certification to Boeing's own staff under its Organization Designation Authorization (ODA) program. Airlines and their crews operate the airplane as its clients, with the procedures and training the design gives them. Other regulators that validate FAA approvals, and the investigators of the two accidents, belong to the environment.

The MAX 8 is a derivative of the 737-800 (Next Generation, NG). Its larger engines changed pitch behaviour at high angle of attack. The NTSB describes Boeing's response as aerodynamic changes plus "a stability augmentation function, MCAS, as an extension of the existing speed trim system" to "improve aircraft handling characteristics and decrease pitch-up tendency at elevated AOA" (ASR-19-01). The KNKT report describes MCAS as a flight control law contained in each of the two flight control computers (FCCs) and active only in the one that is master for the flight; the master is the left computer at power-up and alternates between left and right by flight. Its angle-of-attack input came from a single sensor, on the master computer's side.

As delivered, MCAS acted in manual flight with flaps up when angle of attack exceeded a Mach-dependent threshold, trimming the stabilizer nose down. Pilot trim-switch input stopped it. If the high angle of attack persisted for more than 5 seconds after that reset, it commanded another input (KNKT). To let the stabilizer move against the column, the first officer's column cutout module was modified to inhibit its aft cutout while MCAS is active (KNKT). The design began with a maximum of 0.6 degrees of stabilizer movement. In March 2016 Boeing revised MCAS so that it could command "incremental stabilizer to a maximum of 2.5 degrees at low Mach decreasing to a maximum of 0.65 degrees at high Mach" (KNKT).

## What the system was required to do

Airlines and their pilots relied on the MAX handling like the NG, so that pilots type-rated on the NG could fly it after differences training without a simulator course. The House majority-staff report records that the program's chief project engineer called obtaining Level B (non-simulator) training a "design objective", and that Boeing's launch-customer contract with Southwest would have cost Boeing money had simulator training been required. KNKT states the link from the engineering side: MCAS "was needed in order to make the Boeing 737-8 (MAX) handling characteristics so similar to the NG versions that no simulator training was needed for type rating." A need for training equivalence thus became a requirement on handling, and handling was met by a function the crew's training did not describe. Passengers relied on a certified airplane; the FAA and validating regulators relied on Boeing's certification documents.

## What happened

On 29 October 2018 (Lion Air 610) and 10 March 2019 (Ethiopian 302), a faulty left angle-of-attack reading was followed by repeated automatic nose-down trim after flap retraction. On Lion Air the sensor carried a bias of about 21 degrees, which was not detected when the replacement sensor was installed (KNKT). The same fault also produced a stick shaker, airspeed and altitude disagree alerts and other indications at the same time. The NTSB, writing in 2019 while both investigations were open, concluded that the accident pilots' responses "differed and did not match the assumptions of pilot responses to unintended MCAS operation on which Boeing based its hazard classifications." The account of Ethiopian 302 here rests on the NTSB's recommendation report.

## The certification reasoning

Boeing's functional hazard assessment classified uncommanded MCAS operation as "major". The NTSB notes that this classification indicates a remote probability and effects such as reduced control capability or increased crew workload. The classification rested on assumptions Boeing said it uses across its models, drawn from FAA regulation and guidance (14 CFR 25.671, AC 25-7C): that uncommanded inputs can be countered without exceptional skill by moving the controls in the normal sense, and that the pilot will respond promptly. The guidance's time allowances are a recognition time that "should not normally be less than 1 second" and recovery action not initiated until at least 3 seconds after recognition (KNKT, quoting the advisory circular). The JATR observed that applicants appear to treat guidance meant for test pilots as a design assumption that the pilot responds correctly within 4 seconds, and that "no studies were found that substantiate" the FAA guidance on recognition and reaction time (Observation O2.8-D).

The classification then set what was analysed. KNKT reports that Boeing "did not perform a specific fault tree analysis", and that an architecture with redundant angle-of-attack inputs "could have been considered but was not required". The simulator evaluation induced a stabilizer movement of MCAS's rate and duration, "regardless of underlying cause" (NTSB), so an erroneous angle-of-attack input, with the stick shaker and disagree alerts that accompany it, was never part of what a pilot faced in the test. KNKT adds that the simulator tests never reached the 2.5 degree limit. The JATR found that MCAS "was not evaluated as a complete and integrated function in the certification documents", that certification deliverables were not updated as the function changed, and that a decision during certification to remove MCAS information from the draft Flight Crew Operating Manual left the FAA Flight Standardization Board "not fully aware of the MCAS function".

The angle-of-attack disagree alert was specified as standard, but the delivered software tied it to an optional angle-of-attack indicator, and Lion Air had not bought that option, so the alert did not appear (KNKT). KNKT's contributing factors name the assumptions about crew response, which it calls "consistent with current industry guidelines" but "incorrect", the reliance on a single sensor, the absence of MCAS from manuals and training, and the missing alert.

## Vertical and horizontal structure

The FAA's delegation to Boeing's ODA is a vertical structure in the ordinary sense: staff of the manufacturer find compliance on the FAA's behalf. The JATR reports that the FAA initially delegated acceptance of about 40 percent of the program's certification plans, and later delegated further plans it had first retained. What passed back up was documents, and the JATR findings concern that return path: deliverables not updated as MCAS grew, and an FAA that could not see that MCAS used the stabilizer in a way it had not been used before, to change the column force feel.

Horizontal relations show where the information failed to travel. Boeing's engineering groups, its manual writers, the Flight Standardization Board that sets training, and the operators each depended on others' documents; the omission from the draft manual reached the board and, through it, the crews. No single delegation relation carried that dependence.

## The cockpit

The pilot holds the task of flying and decides among options, with authority over the stabilizer through the trim switches and cutout switches. MCAS is a control law, so it is best described as a mechanism, one that closes a loop from angle of attack to stabilizer trim. It sits beneath the pilot's control as an augmentation function, there to make the MAX present the NG's handling, and both acted on the same stabilizer. The design's composition rule for the two was an assumed pilot reaction, which the sources call an assumption. The trim-switch input meant as the pilot's countermeasure also reset MCAS, so the assumed response and the function's repetition were coupled.

Correspondence is where this reading helps most. The crew held authority over the stabilizer but did not have the information that MCAS existed, that it used one sensor, or that disagreement between sensors went undisplayed on most aircraft. The single angle-of-attack source was accepted at a boundary as correct, with no estimate carrying its uncertainty to anyone who might use it. The sensor was a trusted boundary in the vocabulary's sense; its contract did not include "wrong", though the NTSB notes 25 stick-shaker activations around takeoff in the 737 fleet over 17 years.

## What it shows

A function added to meet one requirement enlarged its authority over a shared control surface, from 0.6 to 2.5 degrees, while the documents, the crew's knowledge and the safety assessment stayed at an earlier state. The sources' "authority" here is the control-engineering sense, the range of travel MCAS may command, and it grew while the crew's knowledge did not. When an automatic function acts on what a person also controls, each side needs to know what the other may do, and the person's assumed response is part of the function's specification. The hazard classification bound construction as a requirement does: it decided whether a fault tree and redundancy were needed and how much documentation the function warranted. It was an assessment of effect under assumed crew behaviour, and it came to stand in for the airplane's actual tolerance of the fault. One premise, that the crew would retrim within seconds, served a family of mechanisms (the classification, the simulator method, the single sensor, the manual content) and was never tested against the case the single sensor made likely.

The sources' own words (function, control law, uncommanded, assumption, hazard classification) describe most of this system without further vocabulary. "Delegation" fits only the FAA and Boeing relation, not the crew and MCAS, to whom nothing was handed.
