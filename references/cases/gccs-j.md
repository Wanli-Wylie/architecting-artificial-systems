# GCCS-J and the common operational picture

The Global Command and Control System-Joint (GCCS-J) is, in the words of CJCSI 3155.01C (24 January 2024), the Chairman's operating policy for it, the Department of Defense "system of record for situational awareness" (SA) and "the DoD system of record for joint C2." The instruction describes "a worldwide system that provides the Joint Staff, CCMDs, Services, Defense Agencies, Joint Task Forces and their Service Components, and other DoD Components with information processing and dissemination capabilities necessary to execute operations," and says it "is the authoritative data source for numerous fielded capabilities and emerging technologies." Its purpose is to provide "a relevant, comprehensive, timely, and accurate global common operational picture (COP)" for the National Military Command System, which must meet presidential and Secretary of Defense requirements, "including uninterrupted processing, display, and exchange of operational information." The system's governance is the Department of Defense's: the instruction applies to the Joint Staff, Services, combatant commands, Defense agencies "and all DoD Components that employ GCCS-J." GCCS-J is more than the picture, and a 2006 DOT&E summary names planning and readiness components beside the one that carries situational awareness. This case follows the picture and the arrangements that keep it running.

## Pictures and sites

The instruction defines the picture by successive products, using definitions that "are not standardized within the DoD and are applicable only within the context of this instruction." A tactical picture is "the real-time or near-real-time integrated battle picture derived from sources managed by a component commander of the Joint Task Force." A TOP common operational picture is a "theater-level COP that is consolidated, curated, and managed by CCMDs for a specified AOR." The Global COP is "the fusion of the CCMDs' TOP COP information to deliver a near-real-time rendering of the worldwide operational environment." The instruction's example of an outage is a step between pictures: "loss of tracks flowing from the common tactical picture (CTP) at the Joint Task Force level or below to the TOP COP is an outage, while use of an alternate/backup communications path to provide required data is not considered an outage."

The system is a set of sites. Combatant commands and Services each "manage and operate GCCS-J sites," the Services providing "necessary resources and support" to their subordinate sites and the combatant commands providing "oversight" of theirs. DISA's program management office supplies the baseline software, patches and training. Critical sites are designated by Joint Staff J-36: the National Military Command Center and its alternate, U.S. Strategic Command headquarters, combatant command headquarters, and other sites that "provide information, data feeds, or other services critical to C2 operations." The instruction says why: "When a critical site's ability to provide data is hampered in any way, the resultant cascading impacts can significantly influence operations at all levels globally." Critical sites carry numbers, among them a rolling annual operational availability of 99.8 percent, and 24/7 monitoring. An outage "may consist of complete or partial loss of hardware, software, or connectivity; increase in latency; or sensor outages that compromise completion, timeliness, and accuracy of the Global COP."

## Two structures

Authority runs down in the instruction's own word, "delegated": the Deputy Director, Joint Staff J-36 "is delegated authority to discharge CJCS responsibilities relevant to this instruction" and serves as the GCCS-J Operational Sponsor, and the Chief of its N2C3 Division is "delegated responsibility for day-to-day oversight and management." Security runs down a second line. The Joint Staff's Principal Authorizing Official for Warfighting Mission Area systems appointed U.S. Strategic Command as the GCCS-J Authorizing Official, who is to "Issue an ATO for the GCCS-J version being deployed." Each site then issues its own authorization to connect, and its site authorizing official "may approve such deviations if the risk is considered manageable and must notify the USSTRATCOM AO." DISA is Information System Owner and Program Manager.

Other relations cross these lines. Each critical site names a site coordinator in writing. The Joint Operations Support Center, which the instruction places among DISA's responsibilities, gives help-desk support, "monitors the operating status of sites consistent with agreements with those sites," and compiles reports for the Joint Staff and the Global COP manager. Sites report unscheduled outages "within 10 minutes of discovery" and update hourly. Where management tools flag a problem and the site is silent, "if the JOSC has not received communication from the site after 10 minutes, it will start calling the site," and unresolved outages go to the National Military Command Center watch floor within two hours. The picture's content has its own management: component commanders for the sources of a tactical picture, combatant commands for a theater picture, and U.S. Strategic Command as Global COP manager under a separate instruction and concept of operations that this one cites and does not restate. U.S. Strategic Command thus fills three roles, authorizing official, Global COP manager and, at its headquarters, critical site, and a combatant command headquarters is at once a site run under the instruction, the manager of a theater picture and a client of DISA.

The two structures do not share a shape: operation and security run down chains of delegation, reporting and monitoring run between sites and the support center, and the picture's content is assembled through management of each picture that follows the command structure. Resources come through the Services and oversight through the combatant commands, a division that resembles the separate Service and combatant command lines the [joint command](joint-command-support.md) case describes. The instruction mostly governs the operation of the system, and says little of how a commander is to use the picture.

## Consumers, data and copies

Data leaves GCCS-J through governed interfaces. The instruction holds that "consumption of GCCS-J COP data by external systems requires coordination and discipline." A requester submits to J-36 the interface's purpose, access controls, transformations and further dissemination, and its approach to timeliness, "with the objective of being able to characterize any deltas from GCCS-J data or displays that might arise as a result." Organizations that are not GCCS-J sites request through a sponsoring site. The reason given is that "the integrity and consistency of COP data throughout the operational environment is essential for effective C2." Consistency is pursued by controlling versions, interfaces and consumers, so that sites "deploy and install identical system version releases."

## Measures and observation

The availability figure is a binding requirement, and the definition of an outage ties it to what the picture is for, since degraded completion, timeliness and accuracy count. Hours are still how it is computed, so a degraded picture enters the figure when someone classes it as an outage, and the treatment of short-notice scheduled outages is "at the discretion of the Joint Staff J-36." The route is not measured, only the arrival of the data: a backup path is not an outage. The system observes itself through site reports, the support center's monitoring and a chat room where outages are posted "for community awareness," and an absence is itself a signal that starts a call.

The picture serves "all echelons," and the joint definition reads "a single identical display of relevant information shared by more than one Command" (the instruction's glossary, citing JP 3-0). Army doctrine, in FM 3-0, quotes ADP 6-0's definition, a display "tailored to the user's requirements and based on common data and information shared by more than one command," and adds that Army forces "require a joint common operational picture (COP) of friendly forces." Army doctrine thus states a requirement on the joint picture, and its own definition is tailored where the joint one is identical.

## What "system of record" says, and what it leaves open

The instruction is explicit that the system as a whole is the "system of record" and "authoritative data source". The phrase tells everyone else where to take their data from. It does not say how a track, or a picture, comes to be. Reading the system by that question, the instruction gives a partial answer and points elsewhere for the rest.

What it states is who manages the sources of each picture: component commanders for the tactical picture, combatant commands for a theater picture ("consolidated, curated, and managed"), and U.S. Strategic Command as Global COP manager under a separate instruction and concept of operations that this one cites and does not restate. What changes a track on its way up is left to those documents. A tactical picture is "derived from sources", a theater picture is consolidated and curated, and the Global COP is formed by "fusion" of theater pictures. The reader is not told which action alters an individual track, who may make that change, whether a person decides or a program computes, or how uncertainty on a track travels with it. Nor is it said what must arrive from a theater for the global picture to count as complete, or what settles two theater pictures that disagree about the same object. The Global COP is described as a rendering, and the rule by which a rendering is assembled and corrected is the part a reader most needs when two displays differ.

This is the more important question, and "system of record" tends to stop it being asked. A system can be the place everyone is told to read from and still have several places where the same thing is changed, with nothing between them to settle a disagreement. What the instruction does with disagreement is aimed at copies. Consumers outside GCCS-J must coordinate with J-36 and characterize "any deltas from GCCS-J data or displays that might arise", and sites "deploy and install identical system version releases". Consistency is pursued by controlling versions, interfaces and consumers, so that the same inputs are handled the same way everywhere, and says nothing of how an input is judged. The case therefore shows an instruction that makes a shared picture a managed, operated and secured thing, with a measure that reaches back to what users need from it, and leaves what it takes for the picture to be right to documents it cites.

## What carries the picture

The picture exists only as software running at sites, on networks, fed by sensors and read by people, and nearly all of the instruction concerns keeping those running. Its principles are those of operating a distributed system. A critical site that cannot provide data produces "cascading impacts" at all levels, so the sites that matter most are designated, monitored around the clock and held to a rolling availability figure. A versioned baseline from DISA keeps sites interchangeable. Latency, sensor outages and loss of connectivity are all ways the picture degrades, and the outage definition includes them for that reason. The route is deliberately not measured, only the arrival of the data, because a backup path serves the picture as well.

People are the other half of the mechanism. A site that is silent is called by telephone after 10 minutes, outages are posted in a chat room "for community awareness", a site coordinator is named in writing, and the National Military Command Center watch floor receives the problems that remain after two hours. These are routines of operators and watch officers, and they are how a system spread across commands finds out it is unwell. Their design reflects the separate lines of authority described above: resources come through the Services and oversight through the combatant commands, so the support center monitors and reports while the sites act.

Ownership is divided by aspect: the Operational Sponsor, the information system owner (of the software baseline), the authorizing official, the Global COP manager and the site coordinators. Each answers for how the system runs or is secured; the instruction names managers for the sources of each picture and no owner for an individual track. Several of its roles are offices with responsibilities and not decision makers, and "fusion" is where a decision maker, human or program, would stand. Where the instruction says little of how a commander is to use the picture, the Army's FM 3-0 supplies a requirement from the user's side, that Army forces "require a joint common operational picture (COP) of friendly forces."
