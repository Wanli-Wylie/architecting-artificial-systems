# Multics to Unix, and the kernel boundary

Multics was announced in 1965 as a computer utility. Corbató and Vyssotsky wrote that the aim was "a computing system which is capable of meeting almost all of the present and near-future requirements of a large computer utility", one that must "run continuously and reliably 7 days a week, 24 hours a day in a way similar to telephone or power systems" and serve everything from interactive users to absentee batch jobs. Its framework had to be "general, and capable of evolving with time". The announced means were a virtual memory of segments and pages, dynamic linking of cross-references at execution time, sharable recursive pure procedures, pooled processors and a file system with automatic backup, and "most of the software of the system will be almost identical in form to user programs". The requirement was broad, and the means were chosen to meet it.

Unix began in 1969 among people at Bell Laboratories who had worked on Multics as the Laboratories withdrew from the project. Ritchie, in his account first presented in 1979, says what they wished to preserve was "not just a good environment in which to do programming, but a system around which a fellowship could form", and that Multics "could support us, albeit at exorbitant cost". The users were the builders at first. In the last half of 1971 three typists from the Patent department used the system for patent applications, and the department became, in Ritchie's words, "the first of many groups at the Laboratories to ratify our work". The 1974 paper by Ritchie and Thompson reports a PDP-11/45 installation on which Unix occupied 42K bytes, "less than two man years" spent on the main system software, and hopes users will find "simplicity, elegance, and ease of use" its most important characteristics. That is a purpose and a hope rather than a stated requirement. The system studied here is one installation at Bell Laboratories, under the Laboratories' management, with its users and their programs as clients.

## The interface and what lies beneath it

What the system offers through its call interface is small and uniform. "The most important job of UNIX is to provide a file system", of ordinary files, directories and special files. Ordinary files have no structure the system knows: "the structure of files is controlled by the programs which use them, not by the system". Each device appears as a special file, which the paper counts a threefold advantage: "file and device I/O are as similar as possible", names have the same syntax, and special files share the protection mechanism. Open and create return a file descriptor; processes come from fork, exec, wait and exit. Protection is an owner, seven protection bits and a super-user. Each directory appears in exactly one other, "to simplify the writing of programs which visit subtrees of the directory structure" and "to avoid the separation of portions of the hierarchy", so the rule against arbitrary links to directories protects a property that programs walking the tree rely on, in every state the file system reaches.

The contract is spare and says where it stops. A read "may without error be less than count". The system "has sufficient internal interlocks to maintain the logical consistency of the file system" but offers no locks, and the authors "take the view that locks are neither necessary nor sufficient, in our environment, to prevent interference between users of the same file". Files and processes are what the interface offers; the machine, drivers and tables beneath realize them. Nothing in that realization takes on a task or chooses among ways of doing it, so the relation between the interface and what lies under it is one of abstraction, and no work is handed down. The sources say "the system" and "the operating system"; Ritchie uses "kernel" once, for the rudimentary code Thompson wrote for the GE-645, and the 1974 paper not at all. The kernel boundary in the title is this case's name for the line the call interface draws.

Programs take the kernel's contract as given, but in the early system the reliance was social as much as technical. On a machine "with no memory protection", Ritchie recalls, "every test of a new program required care and boldness, because it could easily crash the system"; the 1974 paper says the second version ran on "the unprotected PDP-11/20".

## The shell above it

Above the interface sits the Shell, "a program called the Shell" that is "a command line interpreter". Ritchie writes: "Crucial to subsequent development was the implementation of the shell as a user-level program stored in a file, rather than a part of the operating system." The users are the ones who act here. The shell is a program interpreting a line by fixed rules, as the kernel is, and the kernel interprets no commands. The shell handles "<" and ">" itself, so that "no special coding to handle I/O redirection is needed within each command", and Ritchie says the fork-based process control made background processes and recursive use of the shell "trivial to implement". That the command language is "selectable on a per-user basis" (the 1974 paper's list of features) is policy kept apart from mechanism, and is itself a matter of who may change what: the interface commits to what a descriptor and fork do, and what a command line means can change without changing that.

## What Multics contributed and where ownership mattered

The 1974 paper credits Multics for what it took: Multics "suggested the particular form of the I/O system calls" and "both the name of the Shell and its general functions", and the idea that the Shell create a process for each command. Two further choices show who owned a piece of the system deciding where a function could go. Redirection existed in Multics as a command, "iocall attach user_output file xx". Ritchie explains, as his own recollection, why building it into the shell did not arise there: "the implementors of the IO system were at Bell Labs in Murray Hill, while the shell was done at MIT. We didn't consider making changes to the shell (it was their program)." In Unix "both the Unix IO system and its shell were under the exclusive control of Thompson", and the change took "an hour or so". The function is the same in both places. What differed was whether the person who saw that it belonged in the shell could change the shell, and that depended on who held the program, on two sites and two groups, and not on anything in the design of either system.

The pipe came in 1972 at McIlroy's suggestion, "or perhaps insistence". Ritchie argues that Multics spliceable I/O modules were not its precursor because they had to be "specially coded", whereas "the genius of the Unix pipeline is precisely that it is constructed from the very same commands used constantly in simplex fashion". A uniform interface that every command already used made the pipe possible. Here the other kind of change is at work: no one had to be persuaded to alter any command, because the commands already met the one interface a pipe needed.

## The reformulation and its cost

Multics asked for a general utility and answered with segments, sharing and protection among its means. Unix asked what a small group needs to program together on a cheap machine and answered with less. The cost shows in the 1974 paper: program text is shared among processes running the same program, but no segments are shared by name; there is a "linking loader" in place of Multics-style dynamic linking (the PDP-7 system had "no loader or link-editor"); no user-visible locks; and no accounting ("The current version of UNIX avoids the issue by not charging any fees at all"). Ritchie says several unusual features are there "because they represented small, easily-coded changes to what existed", fork being 27 lines of PDP-7 assembly and the separation of fork from exec following from that. A design that grows by what is cheap to add can leave an accident that later gets a reason. The 1974 paper states no availability requirement like Multics's telephone-or-power aim.

Saltzer and Schroeder's least common mechanism, published a year after the 1974 paper, gives a reason for the same placement: implement a new function "as a library procedure that can be handled as though it were the user's own" rather than "a supervisor procedure shared by all users", so that users can substitute their own. Ritchie's own account of the user-level shell stresses accident and convenience.

## What the reading adds

For Multics a requirement is stated, broad and ambitious; for Unix, whose first users were its builders, the sources give a purpose and a hope. The kernel and the shell are not related by delegation: the shell uses the kernel's interface, and the sources' own terms (system call, file descriptor, user-level program, Shell) describe it well. What the case adds is the separation of mechanism from policy, and the place of ownership in deciding where functions were put: a function lands where the person who wants it can reach, and a design that keeps its pieces in the hands of one small group can move a function in an hour. The physical side shows in the same account. A machine with no memory protection made every test of a new program a risk to the whole system. The sources show no horizontal structure among the people using the system beyond users sharing files through the interface.
