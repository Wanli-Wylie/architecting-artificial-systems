# The FAA's air traffic organization

The Federal Aviation Administration's Air Traffic Organization provides air traffic control in the United States. Its governance is carried by two orders that bind its personnel and anyone using its directives: FAA Order JO 7110.65, *Air Traffic Control*, which prescribes procedures and phraseology, and FAA Order JO 7210.3, *Facility Operation and Administration*, which sets how facilities are run. The case studies that organization. Airlines and pilots are its clients, the military and other government agencies its neighbours and users of the same airspace, and other countries' air navigation services its environment. Where U.S. controllers work under foreign rules, the order says so: a deviation is allowed to conform with "ICAO Documents, National Rules of the Air, or special agreements where the U.S. provides air traffic control service in airspace outside the U.S. and its possessions".

## What it is for

The order states its own purpose in 7110.65 paragraph 2-1-1: "The primary purpose of the ATC system is to prevent a collision involving aircraft operating in the system." Further purposes follow, a safe, orderly and expeditious flow of traffic and support to national security and homeland defense, and additional services are owed "when the work situation permits". Paragraph 2-1-2 turns this into a priority for the controller: first priority is "separating aircraft and issuing safety alerts". A second mission belongs to traffic management: "to balance air traffic demand with system capacity to ensure the maximum efficient utilization of the National Airspace System". The two requirements are met in different places: separation by the controller who holds the airspace, balancing by traffic management across facilities.

## The line structure

Facilities answer to the organization's management. Paragraph 2-1-1 of 7210.3 says that "Air Route Traffic Control Centers (ARTCCs) and terminal air traffic facilities are responsible to the respective Air Traffic Service Area office", flight service stations to their own group, and the Command Center "to the Director of NAS Operations". Inside a facility, the air traffic manager issues a standard operating procedure that fixes, among other things, "the jurisdictional boundaries for each operational position/sector". A sector's airspace is therefore a task and an authority at once: the controller who holds it answers for separating the aircraft in it. The pilot in command holds a role set by regulation, not by the order: 14 CFR 91.3 makes the pilot "directly responsible for, and is the final authority as to, the operation of that aircraft".

## A second vertical structure for traffic management

Traffic management does not follow the line. 7210.3 paragraph 18-2-3 says that the Command Center, the David J. Hurley Air Traffic Control System Command Center (ATCSCC), "has been delegated the authority to direct the operation of the TM system", and that "All TMUs must assist the ATCSCC, as directed". The traffic management units (TMUs) it works through sit in the ARTCCs and designated terminal facilities, and a unit's staff are supervised inside their own facility, up through the traffic management officer to the air traffic manager (18-3-2). Each unit "is delegated the authority to direct traffic flows and implement TBM operations and TM initiatives in conjunction with, or as directed by the ATCSCC". The Command Center is "the final approving authority regarding all interfacility TM initiatives" and the approval authority for en route and designated terminal interfacility initiatives; a center or TRACON is responsible for initiatives in its own jurisdiction that cause no reportable delay. The units "continue to retain the latitude to tactically adjust the flow of traffic within their own facilities", such as sector-to-sector miles-in-trail restrictions, so the order writes latitude into the unit's authority and states where the Command Center's approval begins.

Initiatives reach people on the line by being carried out: they are "carried out by air traffic controllers and flight operators". Some bind the airlines through agreements: of the low-altitude arrival and departure routings the order says "LAADR requires a written agreement with the customers prior to implementing". The same paragraph separates this from the sectors' own dealings with each other: "TMIs do not include controller coordinated actions." Two structures with different heads meet in one controller's position, and the order names which one a given action belongs to.

## Horizontal structure between facilities and sectors

Most of what keeps aircraft safe moves sideways. Control of an aircraft passes at "a prescribed or coordinated location, time, fix, or altitude", or when a radar handoff and frequency change are complete and "authorized by a facility directive or letter of agreement which specifies the type and extent of control that is transferred" (2-1-15). 7110.65 defines a handoff as the transfer of radar identification when "radio communications with the aircraft will be transferred", and a point out as a transfer of identification where radio communications are not (5-4-2). The transferring controller completes a handoff before the aircraft enters "the airspace delegated to the receiving controller"; the receiving controller issues needed restrictions before accepting, and radar contact is the reply that grants approval. This is a request and an acceptance between peers, with the receiver able to set conditions, and the aircraft's data block and the handoff displayed on the screens are the shared record. Paragraph 2-1-14 requires coordination before an aircraft enters another controller's area, "unless otherwise specified by a letter of agreement or facility directive".

The letter of agreement (LOA) is the contract form. Paragraph 1-1-10 states: "Procedures/minima which are applied jointly or otherwise require the cooperation or concurrence of more than one facility/organization must be documented in a letter of agreement. LOAs only supplement this order." Any minima in an LOA may not be less than the order's, except where military authority has authorized reduced separation between military aircraft. 7210.3 paragraph 4-3-2 lists subjects: radar handoff procedures between centres, approach control service between a centre and a tower, special VFR operations between a centre or approach control and a flight service station, procedures with other government agencies. An LOA therefore sometimes joins units of the organization and sometimes reaches across its edge to an airport manager, a local operator or another agency. The air traffic manager negotiates it, a facility is expected to review it at least annually, and each agreement is confined to a single subject.

Where agreement fails, the governance supplies a place for it. When "the resolution of procedural or operational problems is not possible, or when a directive is incompatible with another directive or agreement, the facility must notify its higher authority management structure for resolution assistance" (7210.3, 2-1-1).

## Delegation of airspace

The sources use "delegate" for several things. The Command Center is delegated the authority to direct a system, which is delegation in the vertical sense, though the units that carry it out belong to other facilities. A letter of agreement can, in the words of 4-3-3, "Delegate responsibility for control of IFR aircraft" to another facility: it describes the area, the altitudes and conditions, and the clearance limits, reporting points, handoff points and release points. That transfers responsibility for a defined volume of airspace between facilities, approved within the hierarchy and written as an agreement. The receiving controller does not work on the delegator's behalf with the delegator's task and report back; it holds the airspace in its own right while the agreement lasts. This is closer to a transfer of jurisdiction between peers than to the delegation the Command Center receives, and the order's own use of the word covers both.

## How jurisdiction comes to be, and what carries it

At any moment one controller has a given aircraft. The rules of a handoff exist to keep that so: the transferring controller completes the handoff before the aircraft enters the receiving controller's airspace, the receiver sets conditions before accepting, and radar contact is the acceptance. Control passes at a place, time, fix or altitude agreed beforehand, so that only one controller has an aircraft at a time. Who may change this arrangement follows the structure. The air traffic manager fixes the jurisdictional boundaries of sectors in a standard operating procedure. Facilities that share a boundary change the handoff terms by negotiating a letter of agreement, which supplements the order and may not go below its minima. Anything the two cannot settle goes to the higher management. The Command Center changes traffic flows through the units, and not an aircraft's controller.

All of this happens through controllers, radar displays and radio, and the procedures are written around how they behave. A handoff is defined by whether "radio communications with the aircraft will be transferred", a point out by their not being. The data block and handoff shown on the screens are what controllers on both sides see and rely on. The order's procedures and phraseology are written for controllers who must apply them, and the order expects them to use judgment where it is silent. Separation is the controller's own work, which is why the order places it first.

## Describing the design

The order tells readers how to read its verbs (1-2-1). “Must” means a procedure is mandatory, “Should” means a procedure is recommended, “May” means a procedure is optional, and “Will” means futurity, not a requirement for the application of a procedure. A reader, human or model, can then tell a requirement from an expectation and from a permission. The order also places a limit on itself: paragraph 1-1-1 requires controllers to "exercise their best judgment if they encounter situations that are not covered by it", and 2-1-2 notes that no standard list of duty priorities could apply to every situation.

## What the case shows

One governance carries two vertical structures and a dense horizontal one. Line management gives facilities their tasks and answers for the airspace; the Command Center directs traffic management through units that are staffed and supervised in the facilities. Sectors and facilities hand aircraft to one another under agreements that supplement, and may not undercut, the national order, and problems that agreements cannot settle go to a common higher management. The roles of air traffic manager, controller and traffic management coordinator come from the organization's orders, while the pilot in command has a role set by regulation. Public orders leave open how the Command Center's directions and a facility manager's line authority are weighed when they conflict in practice, beyond the rule that unresolved problems go up the facility's management.
