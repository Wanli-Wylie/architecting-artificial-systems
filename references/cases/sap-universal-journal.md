# SAP ERP and the S/4HANA Universal Journal: deriving what used to be stored

An enterprise that runs its accounts payable, accounts receivable, general ledger, cost accounting and asset accounting on SAP software has one system integrating these functions over one database. The enterprise governs it: its finance and IT functions decide who may post, which accounting principles apply, and which custom programs run against the data. SAP is the supplier of the software and of its data model. Auditors, controllers and tax authorities rely on the postings, and the enterprise's own custom programs are clients of the data model, reading and writing tables that SAP defined. This case follows one change SAP made to that data model and what it cost clients. The case concerns a data model and database, and has no vertical or horizontal actor structure to show.

## The classic data model

Plattner describes the classic SAP Financials model in 2014. The actual accounting documents "are recorded in two tables as an accounting document header and its line items." Around them sit "tables that keep track of the total per account" (for example GLT0 for the general ledger and LFC1 for vendors) and tables that "replicate the accounting line items as materialized views with various filters and different sort orders to improve the performance of frequent queries". Open and closed items for vendors, customers and ledger accounts have their own tables, and controlling keeps its own document header and expense line items (COBK, COEP) with totals (COSP). His example is posting a vendor invoice: "fifteen consecutive steps", "ten inserts and five updates", of which only the header and three line items are the accounting document itself.

The reason was speed. Maintaining balances was "the only way we could achieve reasonable performance as calculating the balance on demand would require the expensive summation of all transfers." Plattner names three drawbacks of pre-built aggregates: "a lack of flexibility as they do not respond to organizational changes", "added complexity in enterprise applications" and "increased cost for data insertion as materialized aggregates have to be updated transactionally safe."

## The Universal Journal

In S/4HANA the Universal Journal (table ACDOCA) is where, in SAP Learning's words, the "actual financial accounting (FI) and controlling (CO) data is recorded in one single table". The page calls it the "definitive single source of truth for financial data". SAP Learning continues: "Since all financial data is based on the same line items, no reconciliation between financial accounting and controlling is necessary." It also describes the period-end work being retired: reconciling data between accounting and controlling because of differing detail, object types and design options. SAP PRESS (secondary, adapted from a book by Salmon and Haesendonckx) says data about one business transaction is "no longer chopped up and stored in different application tables but instead stored as a single, richer journal entry", with dimensions such as cost center, profit center and asset as fields of the line item. The ledger field separates documents kept under different accounting principles, and the posting logic is described as unchanged.

The enabling change was in the database, and Plattner's 2014 paper states the premise being given up: "the assumption that we can anticipate the right pre-aggregations for the majority of applications without creating a transactional bottleneck was completely wrong." With data held in memory in columns, "we could execute the queries of all business applications, including reporting, analytics, planning, etc. directly on the transactional line items." Some stored aggregation remains, below the application: a cache of intermediate results that "is maintained by the database and completely transparent to applications."

The paper reports measurements from productive use of what it calls the simplified SAP Financials, an application without transaction-maintained aggregates: vendor-invoice posting "2.5 times faster" than the classic transaction on a disk-based row store; the share of read queries in total execution time rising from 86 to 98 percent over a week of thousands of users' queries; and, in the classic application, "an average of 3.4 inserts or modifications to other tables within the same application module" per line item, against none in the simplified one. The figures come from SAP's systems and from an author who advocated the approach: "I predict that all enterprise applications will be built in an aggregation and redundancy free manner in the future." The paper does not name ACDOCA or the Universal Journal; the two are read together here because they rest on the same premise, that stored aggregates can be dropped.

## Clients that cannot change with the system

Custom programs and standard transactions read the old tables, and SAP kept them working. SAP Knowledge Base Article 3523400 says compatibility views are "required to map tables from the old world (e.g. COSS) to tables of the new S/4HANA world (e.g. ACDOCA) so that old coding can still work without changes." It adds, quoting SAP Note 2000002: "This mapping is typically rather complex, involving a lot of views and dependencies, so there is a significant risk that there are performance regressions compared to the former plain table access." The article lists performance regression, longer execution times and out-of-memory events among the issues, and calls the views "an abstraction layer over the actual database tables". SAP PRESS (secondary) says the index and totals tables are obsolete and "have been replaced by compatibility views to make sure that older custom programs will continue to work". A third-party German whitepaper (abat, secondary) says the views serve read access only and that tables such as COEP no longer exist as tables. SAP PRESS reports that BSEG, the classic line item table, stays relevant for operational processes such as open item management, with ACDOCA holding the full detail.

## How a balance comes to be

Take a vendor's balance and ask how it comes to be. In the classic model it came to be twice over. A posting wrote line items, from which the balance could always be summed, and the same posting updated the vendor's total in LFC1 as one of its fifteen steps. Controlling recorded the transaction again in its own documents and totals, with its own detail and object types. One business event thus changed several stores at once, and keeping them equal was work: the update steps inside every posting, done "transactionally safe", and the reconciliation between accounting and controlling at period end.

In the Universal Journal one action, a posting, changes one thing, the journal's line items, and every balance and every controlling figure is computed from them when someone reads it. There is nothing to reconcile because nothing else is changed. SAP's "definitive single source of truth for financial data" reads well in these terms: it names the one table that postings write and from which finance and controlling both read. The tables beside it fit the same reading. Plan data comes to be by planning rather than by posting, and it has its own table, ACDOCP. Consolidation's ACDOCU is "essentially an aggregation of the entries in the Universal Journal", a derived form that is stored. BSEG stays in use for operational processes such as open item management. A journal free of redundancy is the direction of the design, not a description of every table.

## What the machine put into the model

The totals were in the model because of the machine. Plattner's reason is physical: on a disk-based row store, summing all transfers for every balance was too slow. An accommodation to the cost of reading thus became part of what the system was in its own terms. The totals had table names, standard transactions and custom programs read them, and every posting had to keep them current. Around them grew index tables, update steps and reconciliation, a family of mechanisms serving one premise about cost.

With data held in columns in memory, summing line items became cheap enough to do on every query, and the premise was revised once rather than each mechanism being trimmed. The family went with it, but aggregation did not leave the system. The database's cache of intermediate results is the same kind of stored aggregate, now on the physical side, kept by the database for its own speed, where no posting has to update it and no program can come to depend on its shape. A responsibility changed layers while the SQL interface the applications use stayed. What changed was not whether aggregates are stored but whether the model contains them: an aggregate in the model has to be changed by every posting and can be read by every client, and one below it is the database's own affair. The sources present the whole change as a revised assumption, and the question asked changed with it, from how to keep balances fast to why store balances at all.

Plattner's first drawback points at the same thing from the organization's side. Pre-built aggregates show "a lack of flexibility as they do not respond to organizational changes". A total kept per cost center fixes the organization's present shape into storage, though the enterprise changes its cost centers and profit centers on its own schedule. In the Universal Journal those dimensions are fields of each line item, so a posting carries the dimensions it was made under, and how postings are grouped is decided when they are read. The premise Plattner gave up was wider than speed: it was that the right aggregations could be anticipated for most applications without a transactional bottleneck.

## The views as a contract

The compatibility views are a contract with clients that cannot change in step with the system, and a thin one. SAP commits that old code works "without changes" and states the performance risk and out-of-memory side effects; it commits to no timing or service level. SAP's "abstraction layer" is a layer in the plain sense, offering the old table shape while ACDOCA realizes it. The views are computed from ACDOCA and, by abat's account, serve reads only, so an old program still sees the shape of COSS or COEP, while the only way to change what it sees is a posting. The issues SAP lists for them are the new premise meeting its own limits. Deriving the old shapes on demand spends, in memory and time at each read, what the old tables spent at each posting, and memory is the resource the whole design rests on.

## What it shows

Pre-computation responds to a physical cost, and once it enters the model, the design that grows around it depends on that cost remaining. When the cost changed, the larger gain was removing the family rather than speeding it, and the aggregation still worth having moved below the model. The price, where clients are many and old, is a compatibility layer whose failure modes are those of the new physical premise.

Most of this case needs no talk of actors. Posting programs, views and the database are mechanisms, and the people who decide (controllers, auditors, SAP's product team) appear as stakeholders who set requirements. The enterprise decides who may post, and in this design a posting writes its journal entry to one table.
