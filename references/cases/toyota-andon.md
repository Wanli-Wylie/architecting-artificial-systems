# Toyota andon

The system here is a Toyota assembly line and the arrangements around its stop cord, under the plant's management. Customers, who are the reason for the requirement that defects not flow on, and the plants of other companies that copy the practice lie outside it.

## What Toyota says it is for

Toyota's description of the Toyota Production System names jidoka as the first of its two pillars, "automation with a human touch". When an abnormality occurs, "the machine or equipment can detect the abnormality and stop automatically, or the operator can stop the line by pulling the stop cord themselves." The stated effect is that "This eliminates the outflow of defective products while also making it possible to build quality into processes by clearly detecting abnormalities and preventing them from recurring." The andon is the display: "the andon (problem display board) lights up to notify workers of the abnormality", and on a line without equipment "the andon is set to light up when the stop cord is pulled so that workers can call the person in charge when there is an abnormality, such as poor quality or delay in human work." Toyota adds that such machines come after the work has been done well by hand and its waste, inconsistency and unreasonable demands removed, so that abnormalities in the work can be detected and built into the machine.

Those who rely on it are the worker, who can raise a problem at once and be helped; the supervisor or team leader, who learns which station needs them; the stations downstream, which should not receive a defective unit; and the customer.

## What happens on a pull

The fullest account of the sequence is the fixed-position stop. Toyota's 1992 booklet *The Toyota Production System*, as quoted by Mark Graban, says that "a worker anywhere on the assembly line who notices an abnormality can stop the production flow by pulling on a rope overhead or by pushing a button." This "lights a numbered lamp on a large andon signboard immediately to call the supervisor's attention to the problem, and the line stops when it reaches the next 'fixed position' – the point at the end of one complete job. The supervisor rushes to the station indicated on the signboard and helps correct the problem." The booklet calls the fixed-position stop system "a classic example of jidoka". The Lean Enterprise Institute's lexicon gives the same design: the line stops "not immediately, but after the product reaches a predetermined position", and "if the problem stays unsolved within the cycle, the conveyor stops at the fixed position." Stopping elsewhere would interrupt the work of every other operation and risk errors such as incorrect assembly.

Graban, reporting Liker and Meier's *The Toyota Way Fieldbook*, adds detail that is the Fieldbook's account of a particular line configuration. The pull sounds an alarm and turns on a yellow light, and the window before the fixed position is "maybe 5 to 30 seconds". If the team leader corrects the problem in time, the line never stops at all and the andon returns to green. A stop stays within one segment because of small buffers at the turns of the line, about ten minutes in total, and restarting a segment needs resynchronization, signalled by an audible alarm. Plants and periods differ, and the Fieldbook's numbers are not Toyota's own.

So the popular picture, that any worker stops the whole factory at once, differs from what these sources describe. The worker's pull is a call for help with a bounded response time, and a stop at a defined point if the problem outlasts it. Graban notes that Toyota itself sometimes uses the shorthand.

## Reading it

The worker's authority is to raise an abnormality, and the booklet says it includes stopping the flow. The effect takes place through mechanisms: lamp, signboard, conveyor and fixed position, which turn the pull into a stop at a point that is safe for the stations around it. The worker and the supervisor or team leader (the booklet says supervisor, the Fieldbook account team leader, and the case keeps each source's word) hold tasks and choose within them. What is open to the worker is judging that something is abnormal and pulling; the stop at the fixed position follows a fixed rule.

The relation between worker and team leader is a request for help, answered by the person who is expected to come. It is horizontal in the sense that it follows the station's call and not a task passed down. The andon board is an observation shared by several: the worker raises it, the leader reads where to go, and the stations around can see the state of the line. It records a call, a response and a stop, which is not the same as a defect fixed. The loop from pull to response runs in seconds, matched to a station's work cycle. Toyota's mention of "preventing them from recurring" points to a slower loop.

Several correspondences are visible. The requirement that defects not flow on has someone at the point of work able to signal, a lamp that shows the signal was made, and a place where the constraint across stations takes effect, the fixed-position stop with its buffers. The structure that answers is part of this. Liker and Meier, as reported, say stop-the-line should not be implemented before a responsive team-leader structure exists, since a signal that nobody answers meets no requirement.

Toyota states the aim as building quality into processes by detecting abnormalities and preventing their recurrence, with the stop at the source in place of passing the defect on. The cost the sources show is stopped or slowed lines, buffers, resynchronization and a standing team-leader role. How often a plant pays it depends on the plant.

## What it shows

Authority given to the person with the information works when a response stands behind it. Graban reports a 2007 BBC story: workers at Toyota's Georgetown, Kentucky plant pulled the cord about 2,000 times a week, against about twice a week at a new Ford plant in Dearborn, Michigan with similar hardware, a gap the BBC attributed to "the legacy of generations of mistrust between shop-floor workers and managers." Graban reports Liker's account that Ford had created an offline team-leader role to answer pulls, and that Ford Finance told the plant the role might be eliminated if it did not prove its value. The hardware did not carry the requirement. The response did, and with it how a pull is received. This is a single comparison reported secondhand.

Graban's reading of the booklet is that it describes the equipment and leaves out what makes pulling feel cheap: a supervisor who arrives without irritation, a team that treats a false alarm as a good catch. A procedure carries the rope and lamp; what makes them used is built in the behaviour around them.

The pull count is useful as an indicator: a high count reads as problems made visible, and a low one may mean people have learned what a pull costs them. If pulls were rewarded or penalized, the count would become the goal, which is goal displacement. The sources do not say how Toyota treats the count, so this is judgment.

The fixed-position rule has several reasons at once. It gives the supervisor time, keeps the other stations in step, and avoids mid-task stops, and the buffers keep one segment's stop from reaching the rest. Each has a reason that can be named.

## Where the vocabulary strains

Calling the worker's pull a decision overstates a trained response to a defined abnormality, and the flow is a signal and a response, with no task passed down and composed back. Policy fits the team leader's handling only loosely, since the sources describe practice and not a stated rule apart from the mechanism.
