# Service level objectives in Google SRE

Inside Google, a service's reliability is stated as a number, and the same number settles a recurring disagreement between the people who change a service and the people who keep it running. The system here is Google's own: services, the teams that build and run them, and a monitoring system, under the company's management. Users of a service, inside or outside Google, are its clients, and the sources describe what they notice, not how they are governed.

## The vocabulary of the practice

A service level indicator (SLI) is "a carefully defined quantitative measure of some aspect of the level of service that is provided": request latency, error rate, throughput, availability, durability, correctness. A service level objective (SLO) is "a target value or range of values for a service level that is measured by an SLI", for example "99% of Get RPC calls will complete in less than 100 ms." A service level agreement (SLA) is "an explicit or implicit contract with your users that includes consequences of meeting (or missing) the SLOs they contain." With no explicit consequence for a miss, the objective is an SLO.

## Who takes part

The sources name the participants by their positions. A product team often "acts as the business owner for an application", and "when a product team exists, that team is usually the best resource to discuss the reliability requirements for a service"; without one, "the engineers building the system often play this role". In the error budget practice "Product Management defines an SLO", and "the two teams", product development and SRE, "jointly define a quarterly error budget based on" it. The two are "generally evaluated on different metrics": development "on product velocity", SRE on reliability, with an information asymmetry between them, since developers see the effort of releasing and SREs see the service in production. The book wants decisions not "driven by politics, fear, or hope" but guided by "an objective metric, agreed upon by both sides".

## How the objective is built

An indicator starts from users: "Start by thinking about (or finding out!) what your users care about, not what you can measure." The Workbook divides an SLI into a specification, "the assessment of service outcome that you think matters to users, independent of how it is measured", and an implementation, which adds a way to measure it. It advises writing SLOs around critical user journeys. The book advises few SLOs: "if you can't ever win a conversation about priorities by quoting a particular SLO, it's probably not worth having that SLO."

The target is chosen against the cost of reliability. "An incremental improvement in reliability may cost 100x more than the previous increment", and "a user on a 99% reliable smartphone cannot tell the difference between 99.99% and 99.999% service reliability." The aim is a service "reliable enough, but no more reliable than it needs to be": "we view the availability target as both a minimum and a maximum." The SLO chapter writes the same shape as "lower bound ≤ SLI ≤ upper bound". It warns against a target taken from current performance, which "may lock you into supporting a system that requires heroic efforts to meet its targets", and prefers "a loose target that you tighten".

Since 100% is the wrong target, the gap below it is an error budget, "a rate at which the SLOs can be missed" ("An error budget is just an SLO for meeting other SLOs!"). Uptime "is measured by a neutral third party: our monitoring system". While budget remains, releases proceed. When it is spent, "releases are temporarily halted while additional resources are invested in system testing and development", and as the budget drains "the product developers themselves will push for more testing or slower push velocity". The book adds that "this outcome relies on an SRE team having the authority to actually stop launches if the SLO is broken."

## The written policy

The Workbook holds that "to make error budget enforcement decisions, you need to start with a written policy" that covers the actions to take and who takes them. Getting it approved by the product manager, the development team and the SREs is "a good test for whether the SLOs are fit for purpose": SREs can argue for relaxing an objective they cannot defend without toil, developers and the product manager can argue for relaxing one that would stall features, and the product manager must understand that a lower SLO also lowers the situations SREs respond to. The Workbook's example policy, for a fictional game service, says "halting change is undesirable; this policy gives teams permission to focus exclusively on reliability when data indicates that reliability is more important than other product features." When the budget is exceeded, "we will halt all changes and releases" until the service is back within its SLO, with exceptions that keep the measure tied to what it measures: feature work may continue if the outage came from a company-wide problem or from users outside the SLO's scope, such as load tests. Disputes over computing the budget or the actions it defines go "to the CTO to make a decision."

## The objective as a limit

Chubby, Google's lock service, was reliable enough that service owners "began to add dependencies to Chubby assuming that it would never go down". "SRE makes sure that global Chubby meets, but does not significantly exceed, its service level objective": if no real failure has brought availability below the target in a quarter, "a controlled outage will be synthesized by intentionally taking down the system" to "flush out unreasonable dependencies". The SLO chapter generalizes it: "users build on the reality of what you offer, rather than what you say you'll supply." The stated objective is what clients may rely on, and the practice acts to keep observed behaviour from replacing it.

The indicators are revisited too. The Workbook says that "before you can improve your SLO targets, you need a source of information about user satisfaction", and suggests counting manually discovered outages and support tickets and checking that periods of steep budget loss and of known outage correspond.

## Reading it in the vocabulary

The SLO is a requirement stated as a measure: a quantity, a bound and a window in users' terms. Its bound is a ceiling as well as a minimum, since the target is "both a minimum and a maximum". Allocated to a service it becomes part of the service's contract with its clients, and an SLA is that contract with consequences attached. The SLI is the measure's observation side, and the specification-implementation split is the one between a requirement and the means that meet it: the implementation can change while the specification holds. By agreement, the monitoring system's measurement is the source of truth for whether the objective was met (the book's "neutral third party"), and a dispute over computing the budget goes to the CTO.

The error budget policy is where the vocabulary helps most. Whether to release or hold is a choice each team's task leaves open, and the policy settles how it is made, as a rule kept apart from the mechanism of releasing and changeable without changing what the SLO means. The sources use "policy" for a written, approved document that also names owners, exceptions and an escalation path, which is the vocabulary's sense and more. Authority sits where the information is: developers see the budget and manage their own risk, SRE holds the authority to stop launches, and the CTO decides disputes. The loop runs over days to weeks, with the revision of indicators against user reports a slower loop around it.

## What it shows and where the vocabulary strains

A measure can be a requirement and also a coordination device. One number, derived from what users care about and not what is easy to count, agreed in advance, measured by something neither team controls and tied to a written consequence, lets two teams with different incentives act consistently without negotiating each release. The indicator stays tied to what it indicates: few SLOs, user journeys and comparison with tickets are aimed at keeping a metric from replacing user experience, and the policy's exceptions do the same, since budget used by out-of-scope load is not what the objective measures.

The structure here is mostly horizontal: product management, development and SRE relate through an agreed objective and policy, with escalation upward for disputes. The teams are actors with tasks, and the SLO, SLI and budget are measures and rules. The sources say "business owner", "product team", "customers" and "users" for what the vocabulary calls stakeholders and clients, and use "clients" itself for the callers of infrastructure services.
