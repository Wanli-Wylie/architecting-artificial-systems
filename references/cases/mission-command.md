# Mission command

Mission command is the US Army's way of commanding a force when the situation will not hold still. It is doctrine, written for commanders and staffs at every echelon, and nothing in it is software. The system studied here is one Army force under its chain of command and doctrine: a corps, division or brigade with its subordinate units and staffs. Its governance is the Army's law, regulation and doctrine, and the orders of the common superior that task-organizes the force. The force sits within the one order the [joint command](joint-command-support.md) case describes, and when it serves in a joint task force, joint doctrine's command relationships apply above it. The enemy, allies and civil authorities are the environment; allies' command authority "is normally negotiated between the participating nations" (FM 3-0, B-36) and lies outside this order.

The case is chosen because the doctrine says, in plain terms, how a task is handed down with room to choose, what bounds that room, what coordinates units that are not each other's subordinates, and when tighter control is wanted instead. The wording of the 2012 edition of ADP 6-0 and ADRP 6-0 is quoted where it is the clearest statement and is marked as the superseded edition. FM 3-0, *Operations* (2022), shows current usage: mission command is "the mission command approach to C2", and "The C2 system includes people, processes, networks, and command posts."

## Delegation: intent, mission orders, initiative

ADP 6-0 (2012) defines mission command as "the exercise of authority and direction by the commander using mission orders to enable disciplined initiative within the commander's intent to empower agile and adaptive leaders in the conduct of unified land operations." Its reason lies in the work: "Operations require responsibility and decisionmaking at the point of action." ADRP 6-0 gives the shape in a phrase, "centralized intent and dispersed execution through disciplined initiative", and says the philosophy works "by reducing the amount of certainty needed to act".

Three pieces carry the structure. The commander's intent is, in FM 3-0, "a clear and concise expression of the purpose of an operation and the desired objectives and military end state", and "succinctly describes what constitutes success for the operation." Mission orders "emphasize to subordinates the results to be attained, not how they are to achieve them". Disciplined initiative is action "in the absence of orders, when existing orders no longer fit the situation", and ADRP says "the commander's intent defines the limits within which subordinates may exercise initiative." Subordinates owe a report back: they "inform their superiors as soon as possible when they have deviated from orders." Delegation nests: commanders "establish their own commander's intent within the intent of their higher commander", and FM 3-0 has commanders communicate intent "two echelons down".

Authority and responsibility are delegated differently. FM 3-0 says that "commanders may delegate authority to accomplish missions", yet "may not absolve themselves of the responsibility for the accomplishment of these missions", and that authority "is never absolute; the extent of authority is specified by the establishing authority, directives, and law." Risk travels in the same channel: "Commanders delegate risk acceptance to subordinates through their commander's intent and other guidance."

## Horizontal structure

An Army force is not only a tree of command. Task organization, in FM 3-0, "allocates assets to subordinate commanders and establishes command and support relationships", and Army support relationships, which "are not a command authority", let one unit serve another without subordination; the joint command case sets them beside the joint terms they differ from.

Mission orders count on horizontal coordination. ADP 6-0 (2012) says they "seek to maximize individual initiative, while relying on lateral coordination between units and vertical coordination up and down the chain of command." Control measures, such as boundaries and graphics, keep neighbouring units from interfering, and 2012 doctrine tells commanders to use "the minimum number of control measures necessary". Commander's critical information requirements, tied to decision points, "help subordinate leaders prioritize allocation of information collection assets and other limited resources", and leaders "maintain a common operational picture with their counterparts in higher and lower echelons." Coordinating authority, the joint term FM 3-0 reproduces, lets its holder "require consultation between the agencies involved", without "the authority to compel agreement", and "in the event that essential agreement cannot be obtained, the matter shall be referred to the appointing authority" (B-15).

## How the task and its edge come to be

A mission order is written by a commander for the subordinate who will carry it out, and the commander writes the intent inside the intent of the echelon above. So the room a subordinate has is made at each level by the person above and bounded by the person above that: no one can widen their own latitude, and a subordinate who sees that existing orders "no longer fit the situation" may act, but owes the report back. Authority is changed by the establishing authority, directives and law; responsibility is not changed by delegating at all. Each unit is a commander and staff acting for the echelon above and containing commanders for the echelon below, and "echelon" is the doctrine's word for a level that carries the chain of command and legal responsibility together.

The intent is both the edge of the subordinate's choice and the standard by which what comes back is read. What returns up the chain is read against purpose and end state, not against every subtask having ended. This lets a unit be replaced, lost or surprised without the larger task becoming undefined, and FM 3-0 asks the same of the people in the structure: "If an echelon of command is destroyed or otherwise made mission ineffective during combat operations, a subordinate leader must assume command as quickly as possible."

Observation runs in two directions. A common operational picture and reports serve decisions at several echelons, and shared understanding gives subordinates the commander's reasons where no message arrives: "the previously developed shared understanding enables subordinate leaders to take effective action on their own while still working towards their commander's end state."

Command and control are two ways of holding one relation. Control is "the regulation of forces and warfighting functions to accomplish the mission in accordance with the commander's intent" (ADP 6-0, 2012), carried by staffs, procedures, control measures and displays. FM 3-0 says "Command is more art than science": how much latitude, to whom and with what risk retained is a commander's judgment, which the doctrine does not reduce to a rule.

## What carries it: people, trust and practice

Everything above happens through people, and most of what the doctrine says about them is about how they behave. A subordinate chooses as the commander would only if the subordinate holds the commander's reasons, and that is built over time, through training, dialogue and shared experience. Trust, command climate, presence and leadership are the physical side of delegation by intent, and the doctrine counts them part of the design: "Ultimately, the solution to degraded communications is mission command and training." ADP 6-0 (2012) also speaks of personal authority, built by a commander's conduct and not delegated; it lives in the people and is not in any order. The enemy, a force with tasks of its own, makes the situation unpredictable, and much of why the doctrine exists is that opposition. Networks and command posts are part of what carries the C2 system too, and when they degrade, what the people hold in common is what keeps the force acting together.

These principles of people and organizations bound the design. Reasons held in advance mean the cost of delegation is paid before the operation, so the reason for pushing decisions down is where information is and how fast it changes, not that the higher commander could not decide. A force whose commanders have not trained together, or whose climate punishes deviation, has the same orders on paper and a different force in the field.

## Tighter control, and neighbours

The doctrine keeps a route back to tighter control, framed as a judgment per phase and per risk. ADRP gives the example of an air assault, whose air movement and landing phases "require precise control and synchronization" while the ground maneuver plan "may require less detail." FM 3-0 names the cost on both sides: "Excessive synchronization can lead to too much control, which limits the initiative of subordinates and undermines mission command", while "ill-advised initiative can be costly" where synchronization is critical. Initiative is also bounded by the whole: "Neither commanders nor subordinates are independent actors when exercising initiative."

Delegation and horizontal coordination do separate jobs here, in a reading drawn from the doctrine rather than stated by it: intent bounds a subordinate's choice, and the common picture, control measures and support relationships keep neighbours' choices compatible. Delegation by intent suits units that are nearly independent; where they are not, the doctrine adds control measures and support relationships, not further delegation. "Establishing authority" and "coordinating authority" name roles and relationships, quoted as the doctrine's.
