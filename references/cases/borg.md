# Borg at Google

Borg is the cluster manager Google runs its own workloads on. The paper describes its users as "Google developers and system administrators (site reliability engineers or SREs) that run Google's applications and services", who submit jobs, each of "one or more tasks that all run the same program". A job runs in one cell, "a set of machines that are managed as a unit", and "Most Borg cells are shared by thousands of users". Everything sits inside one company's governance. The users are its clients, the teams behind Gmail, web search or Bigtable among them, and the machines are the company's own fleet, whose capacity is planned outside Borg. The case follows how a shared pool of machines is divided among teams, and what each part of Borg is for in that division.

## Four separate things

The paper takes care to keep apart what the pool's division consists of. Priority, quota, admission control and scheduling are different mechanisms with different homes.

Priority ranks work. "Every job has a priority, a small positive integer", and "A high-priority task can obtain resources at the expense of a lower-priority one, even if that involves preempting (killing) the latter." The priority bands are "non-overlapping" and run, in decreasing order, "monitoring, production, batch, and best effort". The bands rank kinds of work, not teams: a team's batch analysis and another team's batch pipeline sit in one band, and a team that runs both a serving job and a batch job spans two. To avoid preemption cascades, "we disallow tasks in the production priority band to preempt one another", so within that band priority never takes resources from a peer.

Quota bounds what a user may ask for. It "is expressed as a vector of resource quantities (CPU, RAM, disk, etc.) at a given priority, for a period of time (typically months)", for example "20 TiB of RAM at prod priority from now until the end of July in cell xx". It is checked at admission: "Quota-checking is part of admission control, not scheduling: jobs with insufficient quota are immediately rejected upon submission." Quota is also priced and limited by priority. "Higher-priority quota costs more than quota at lower-priority", and "Production-priority quota is limited to the actual resources available in the cell, so that a user who submits a production-priority job that fits in their quota can expect it to run, modulo fragmentation and constraints." Lower bands are oversold: "every user has infinite quota at priority zero", though this "is frequently hard to exercise because resources are oversubscribed", and a low-priority job "may be admitted but remain pending".

Scheduling places admitted work. The scheduler scans the pending queue "from high to low priority, modulated by a round-robin scheme within a priority to ensure fairness across users", checks which machines are feasible, scores them, and preempts lower-priority tasks if the chosen machine lacks room. It does not decide who may submit what, or what quota costs.

Where quota comes from lies outside all three. "Quota allocation is handled outside of Borg, and is intimately tied to our physical capacity planning, whose results are reflected in the price and availability of quota in different datacenters." Borg enforces quotas and records usage (its Infrastore data serves "usage-based charging, debugging job and system failures, and long-term capacity planning"), but it is not where quota is priced or granted.

So Borg coordinates the division of resources through quotas, priorities, admission control and scheduling, with quota pricing linked to capacity planning. Allocation here is a horizontal structure: its rules, quota against priority, apply to teams alike and do not follow the teams' reporting lines or the owners of jobs. Whatever delegation exists among Google's teams lies outside Borg. Within Borg, a user's relation to the system is a service relation, in which users submit jobs, update them, and read why a job is pending, and Borg decides where tasks run.

## Reservation and reclamation

Quota and limits are promises about the most a job may use. Usage is lower, and the paper measures the gap: in a representative cell, prod jobs "are allocated about 70% of the total CPU resources and represent about 60% of the total CPU usage", and about 55% of memory against about 85% usage. Borg closes part of the gap by estimation: "we estimate how many resources a task will use and reclaim the rest for work that can tolerate lower-quality resources, such as batch jobs. This whole process is called resource reclamation. The estimate is called the task's reservation, and is computed by the Borgmaster every few seconds, using fine-grained usage (resource-consumption) information captured by the Borglet." The reservation starts at the limit and, after 300 s, "decays slowly towards the actual usage plus a safety margin", and "is rapidly increased if the usage exceeds it."

The estimate is read differently by different work. The scheduler uses limits for the feasibility of prod tasks, which therefore "never rely on reclaimed resources and aren't exposed to resource oversubscription", while for non-prod tasks it uses "the reservations of existing tasks". The estimate can be wrong, and the machine then handles it locally: if a machine "runs out of non-compressible resources, the Borglet immediately terminates tasks, from lowest to highest priority, until the remaining reservations can be met", while for compressible ones it throttles. The paper reports that segregating prod and non-prod work "would need 20–30% more machines in the median cell", which is what reclamation and shared cells are worth to the company.

## Structure inside Borg

A cell has "a logically centralized controller called the Borgmaster" and a "Borglet" agent on each machine. The Borgmaster is "logically a single process but is actually replicated five times", with one elected master per cell holding the state in a Paxos-based store. The scheduler is a separate process that "operates on a cached copy of the cell state" and tells the master its assignments; "The master will accept and apply these assignments unless they are inappropriate (e.g., based on out of date state)", which the paper compares to Omega's optimistic concurrency. The master polls each Borglet, and a Borglet "continues normal operation even if it loses contact with the Borgmaster". Already-running tasks keep running without the master. The paper reports that the Borgmaster, "originally designed as a monolithic system", became "more of a kernel" around services for admission control, autoscaling, re-packing, cron and workflow management, and describes Kubernetes as going further, with small controllers that are clients of an API server.

Read as a question of who may change what, the arrangement is this. The state of the cell, which jobs exist and which task sits on which machine, changes only at the elected master, and everything else proposes. The scheduler, a separate process, works from a copy, and the price of working from a copy is that the copy can be old when the proposal arrives. The master settles that by refusing proposals that no longer fit the state it holds, so a placement takes effect only when the master applies it. The machine is the other end of the same arrangement: the Borglet keeps its tasks running when the master cannot be reached, and on a machine that runs out of non-compressible resources it terminates tasks from lowest to highest priority without waiting to be told. Some changes therefore happen at the machine, under rules the master has already set (the priorities), because only the machine knows at that moment that memory has run out.

Who may do what to a job is settled at the master: "Borg has a capability system that gives special privileges to some users", such as letting administrators delete or modify any job. Users' jobs have owners, and what an owner may do is a matter of that system together with quota. A user changes a job by submitting an update, and the master, with its checks and its capability system, decides whether and how it takes effect.

## What it shows

The case shows an allocation structure built from separate parts with separate homes: a ranking by kind of work, a bound per user, an admission check, a placement procedure and an estimate of real use, with the pricing of the bound kept in capacity planning, outside the system that enforces it. That separation lets Borg's rules stay stable while the company's capacity and its quota prices move. The paper also notes a consequence: the use of quota "reduces the need for policies like Dominant Resource Fairness".

The machines set the terms throughout. A machine has a fixed amount of memory and processor, and some of it can be shared out by throttling while some cannot. The line the paper draws between compressible and non-compressible resources decides what the Borglet does when its estimate proves wrong: throttle, or kill. The scheduler's cached copy goes with a master that holds the cell's state in one place, and the refusal of stale proposals is what makes working from that copy safe. The reservation is an estimate computed from what the Borglet measures, and prod and non-prod work read it differently, which is how one number serves two promises. The paper's "user" covers both people and Borg jobs that submit jobs, so a client here may be another system.
