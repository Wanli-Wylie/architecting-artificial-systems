# US joint command and support relationships

The US armed forces are organized under one governance: the authority of the President and the Secretary of Defense, set out in law (Title 10 of the US Code) and in joint doctrine. This case reads how that order gives commanders authority over forces and how it lets commanders aid one another without commanding one another. Two doctrines are involved, and their terms differ. Joint doctrine (Joint Publication 1, JP 1) is written for the whole force. Army doctrine (FM 3-0, Appendix B) takes the joint terms as given for joint forces and adds its own for task-organizing Army units. Allies, other US departments and agencies, and state governors are outside this governance and appear in the doctrine as the environment a commander deals with.

## Two branches, graded authority

JP 1 (2013, with Change 1 of 2017) says that "the President and SecDef exercise authority, direction, and control of the Armed Forces through two distinct branches of the chain of C2." One branch runs through the Secretary of Defense to the combatant commanders "for missions and forces assigned to their commands". For other purposes the chain runs to the Secretaries of the Military Departments and, as they prescribe, to the commanders of Service forces, who exercise administrative control (ADCON) through the Service Chiefs. The doctrine calls this branch "separate and distinct from the branch of the chain of command that exists within a CCMD." So one governance carries two vertical structures, and a Service component command sits in both: it answers to its combatant commander for operations and to its Military Department for administration and support.

Authority passes down the operational branch in named grades, which joint doctrine calls command relationships. Combatant command (command authority), COCOM, belongs only to the commanders of combatant commands, is set by Title 10, USC, Section 164, and "cannot be delegated or transferred." Operational control (OPCON) is the authority to organize and employ forces, assign tasks and direct operations, and it "does not include authoritative direction for logistics or matters of administration, discipline, internal organization, or unit training." Tactical control (TACON) is "limited to the detailed direction and control of movements and maneuvers within the operational area", and "does not provide the authority to give or change the function of the subordinate commander." Each grade is a bounded part of the one above, so a delegation carries a stated amount of authority and the rest stays where it was. The doctrine adds that "unity of command requires that two commanders may not exercise the same command relationship over the same force at any one time", and that "forces, not command relationships, are transferred between commands", with the relationship the gaining commander will hold specified at the transfer.

Joint doctrine also states how much latitude the vertical structure should leave. It says unity of effort over complex operations comes "through decentralized execution of centralized, overarching plans or via mission command", and that "the level of control used will depend on the nature of the operation or task, the risk or priority of its success, and the associated comfort level of the commander." The [mission command](mission-command.md) case shows that delegation inside a force.

## Support across the branches

Alongside the grades sits a different kind of relationship. JP 1 says: "Support is a command authority. A support relationship is established by a common superior commander between subordinate commanders when one organization should aid, protect, complement, or sustain another force." The Secretary of Defense uses it between combatant commanders, and joint force commanders between their subordinate commanders. The two commanders may have no command authority over each other at all, and they can be at any echelon: a commander may support in one operation and be supported in another, and relationships can change by phase.

The doctrine describes a division of work. The establishing authority, the common superior, issues an establishing directive that normally specifies the purpose of the support relationship, the effect desired and the scope of the action to be taken, and should state the forces and resources allocated, relative priority and "the degree of authority granted to the supported commander over the supporting effort." Unless the directive limits it, the supported commander exercises "general direction of the supporting effort": designating and prioritizing targets or objectives and setting timing and duration. The supporting commander "determines the forces, tactics, methods, procedures, and communications to be employed in providing this support" and must "ascertain the needs of the supported force and take action to fulfill them within existing capabilities". When the supporting commander cannot meet the supported commander's needs, either one notifies the establishing authority, who "is responsible for determining a solution." Where one supporting component serves several supported commanders, the directive says "who supports whom, when, and with what prioritization", and "the CCDR having COCOM of the component commanders will adjudicate" conflicts.

Support resembles a service in that the supporting commander chooses forces and methods to meet the supported commander's needs, but its responsibility comes from the common superior's directive rather than from the supporter's own acceptance, and the common superior is where unresolved matters go and who sets priority. JP 1 calls the support relationship "by design, a somewhat vague but very flexible arrangement", and the directive is where a specific case is made precise.

Two further joint arrangements are outside the command relationships. Coordinating authority lets a commander or individual coordinate specific activities involving two or more Military Departments or components. The holder "has the authority to require consultation between the agencies involved but does not have the authority to compel agreement", and FM 3-0 adds the way out: "In the event that essential agreement cannot be obtained, the matter shall be referred to the appointing authority." The doctrine calls it "a consultation relationship between commanders, not an authority by which command may be exercised." Between nations, outside this governance, JP 1 notes that its use "requires agreement among participants, as the commander exercising coordinating authority does not have the authority to resolve disputes." Direct liaison authorized lets a commander's subordinate consult or coordinate directly, within or outside the command.

## Army terms

FM 3-0 gives the joint list (COCOM, OPCON, TACON, support) and then says Army relationships "are similar but not identical to joint command authorities and relationships." Army command relationships are organic, assigned, attached, OPCON and TACON, and they differ from the joint ones partly because they also settle ADCON: attachment moves sustainment, training and unit reporting to the receiving headquarters, while OPCON and TACON leave ADCON where it was. Army support relationships are direct support, reinforcing, general support-reinforcing and general support, and FM 3-0 says they "are not a command authority", in contrast to the joint statement above. Commanders use them "when subordination of one unit to another is inappropriate", for example when a brigade supports a battalion or one unit serves several and needs priorities set, and a unit in direct support keeps its command relationship with its parent while the supported unit sets its priorities of support. It also notes that "Joint doctrine considers direct support a mission rather than a support relationship", and that a joint support relationship is not used when an Army commander task-organizes Army forces to support another Army force. A reader of a staff paper who meets "support" or "direct support" needs to know which doctrine is speaking.

## Reading it in the vocabulary

The vertical structure is delegation of authority in grades, whose extent, in FM 3-0's words, "is specified by the establishing authority, directives, and law". The horizontal structure is the support relationship, with coordinating authority and direct liaison for cases where consultation is all that is wanted. They need not follow each other's shape, and the doctrine builds in the difference: a supporting commander may be senior to the supported one, and a supported commander gets direction of the effort without any claim to the supporter's forces or administration. Roles are the doctrine's (joint force commander, component commander, supported and supporting commander, establishing authority), and the establishing directive states how much authority each holds. The vocabulary's "task" only partly matches the doctrine's mission, task and function, and "authority" is used in the doctrine's own sense, which is the vocabulary's.

## Where the vocabulary strains

"Governance" fits the order the President and Secretary of Defense maintain, but the doctrine's "authority" appears in "establishing authority" and "coordinating authority" as role names, not in the vocabulary's sense. "Support" is a command authority in joint doctrine and is not in Army doctrine, so the word belongs to neither side of the vocabulary and is kept as the source's. Doctrine's "level" and "echelon" are quoted as the source's.
